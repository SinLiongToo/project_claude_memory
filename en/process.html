Process: billions of capacitors and skyscrapers on silicon

Memory pushes semiconductor processing to its limits: DRAM needs capacitors with aspect ratios of several tens to one at a pitch of just over ten nanometres; 3D NAND etches through three hundred layers in one go. Competition between memory makers each generation often comes down to a handful of critical process steps.

Unit process modules

Logic or memory, fabs combine the same set of unit processes again and again. A DRAM takes roughly a thousand steps and dozens of masks.

The DRAM process flow

  1. Active areas and STI: define diagonal island-shaped active areas with multi-patterning (SAQP) or EUV, etch trenches and fill them with oxide.
  2. Buried word line (BCAT): etch word-line trenches into silicon, grow gate oxide, fill with TiN/W (or a dual-work-function TiN + poly gate to cut GIDL), recess below the silicon surface and cap.
  3. Bit-line contact and bit line: open a contact on the active area between two word lines, deposit poly / metal and form the bit line (W, Ru, etc.) with insulating spacers (air gaps or low-k spacers to cut CBL).
  4. Storage-node contacts (BC / SNC) and landing pads (LP): self-aligned holes between bit lines reach both ends of the active area; landing pads rearrange the contacts into a honeycomb that suits capacitor placement.
  5. Capacitor module: deposit a thick mold and support layers → etch high-aspect-ratio holes → deposit the TiN bottom electrode → remove the mold (keeping the support mesh) → ALD high-k → top electrode.
  6. Periphery: planar transistors for sense amps, decoders and I/O; new high-speed DRAM adopts HKMG in the periphery.
  7. Back-end metal: typically 3–4 metal layers (far fewer than logic’s dozen) to save cost.

The capacitor: the hardest part of DRAM

Cell area shrinks every generation, but capacitance cannot (a few fF to 10+ fF), or the signal becomes too small. With C = ε·A/d there are three levers:

  • Taller (A): from planar to trench to stacked capacitors, and now cylinder / pillar capacitors about 1–1.5 µm tall and only tens of nm across — aspect ratios of several tens to one. The etch must be straight without bowing or tapering, and the tall thin structures must not collapse when drying after wet steps (leaning) — hence support layers.
  • Higher dielectric constant (ε): ZrO2/Al2O3/ZrO2 (ZAZ) stacks, with the Al2O3 layer suppressing leakage. Higher-k candidates such as TiO2 and SrTiO3 trade leakage and band gap.
  • Thinner (d): equivalent oxide thickness (EOT) is already below about 1 nm; thinner and leakage rises steeply.

Lithography and DRAM node names

DRAM generations are named 1x, 1y, 1z, 1α (1a), 1β (1b), 1γ (1c), roughly matching half-pitches just over ten nanometres, shrinking about 1 nm per generation. Dense repeating patterns suit self-aligned multi-patterning (SADP / SAQP), at the cost of more masks and steps. From 1z through 1γ, makers moved critical layers to EUV at different times to cut multi-patterning steps and overlay error.

Key point　DRAM scaling is no longer limited by line width but by the capacitor and the access transistor: the capacitor must keep its capacitance and the transistor must keep low leakage and enough drive in a smaller footprint. That is driving the structural changes below.
Micron note　Micron’s DRAM nodes: 1α (2021) and 1β (2022) used multi-patterning without EUV; 1γ (1-gamma, 2025) is Micron’s first DRAM node with EUV on critical layers, and public sources name the Hiroshima fab as one of its production sites. It is a textbook case of “adopt EUV late and hold costs with multi-patterning”.(Compiled from public sources, not official; see Micron’s own announcements for details.)

Next-generation DRAM: 4F² and 3D DRAM

  • 4F² vertical-channel transistor (VCT): the access transistor stands vertically, with the word line around or beside a pillar channel, the bit line buried below and the capacitor on top, cutting the cell from 6F² to 4F². The periphery may be built on another wafer and hybrid-bonded (as in 3D NAND).
  • 3D DRAM: like 3D NAND, stack horizontal 1T1C cells in many layers. The hard part is that every layer needs both a transistor and a capacitor: silicon channels come from Si/SiGe epitaxial stacks, and capacitors must be built sideways.
  • Capacitor-less DRAM: e.g. 2T0C gain cells using the extremely low off-current of oxide-semiconductor (IGZO) transistors, storing data on the read transistor’s gate capacitance.

The 3D NAND process flow

  1. Stack deposition: alternate tens to hundreds of SiO2 / Si3N4 (ON) pairs, each tens of nm thick, with extremely uniform thickness and stress or the wafer warps.
  2. Channel-hole etch: etch through the whole stack in one go — several µm deep, about 100 nm wide, aspect ratio > 50:1 — with tens of billions of holes per wafer whose top/bottom diameter, bowing and twisting must be controlled. With too many layers, the stack is split into 2–3 decks etched separately and aligned.
  3. Hole liner: ALD blocking oxide, SiN trap layer, tunnel oxide and poly channel in turn, with an oxide core (macaroni channel).
  4. Staircase: repeated “trim the resist + etch one layer” builds the steps.
  5. Slit etch and replacement gate: etch long slits through the array, remove all sacrificial SiN through them with hot phosphoric acid, fill W by ALD (newer generations use Mo: lower resistance, no barrier) to form word lines, then build the common source in the slit.
  6. Contacts and back end: hundreds of word-line contacts of different depths in the staircase, bit lines, metal layers.
  7. CMOS integration: build the periphery first and stack the array on top (CuA / PUC), or build them on two wafers and join with hybrid bonding (CBA, e.g. Xtacking), sparing the periphery the array’s high-temperature steps for better performance.

3D NAND scaling directions

  • Vertical: from 24, 32, 64, 96, 128, 176 layers to 200–300+, while thinning each layer to limit total height.
  • Lateral: tighter channel-hole pitch, fewer slits, denser hole patterns.
  • Logical: more bits per cell, TLC → QLC → PLC.
  • Architecture: CuA / CBA moves the periphery under the array or onto another wafer to raise array area share.
  • Materials: word lines W → Mo, better channel mobility, lower-leakage trap layers.
Micron note　Micron’s NAND generations: 176 layers (2020, replacement gate + charge trap + CuA) → 232 layers (2022) → ninth generation G9 (2024, 276 layers per public sources). Beyond layers, each generation also raised interface speed and plane count (e.g. six planes at 232 layers), matching the TLC example in the Spec Designer.(Compiled from public sources, not official; see Micron’s own announcements for details.)

SRAM in logic processes

SRAM shares the logic process and follows its transistor evolution: planar → FinFET → GAA nanosheet → forksheet and CFET (NMOS and PMOS stacked) in development. FinFET fin counts must be integers, which limits cell-ratio tuning; GAA channel widths are continuous, restoring SRAM design freedom.

After 5 nm SRAM nearly stopped shrinking, because of metal-pitch and contact limits, Vt variation and the minimum sizes needed to hold Vmin. Backside power delivery moves power rails to the back of the wafer, freeing front-side routing and helping SRAM density.

Back-end integration of emerging memories

  • MRAM: between two metal layers, PVD deposits a dozen ultrathin films (seed layer, synthetic antiferromagnetic reference, CoFeB, ~1 nm MgO, free layer, cap), then ion-beam etch (IBE) defines pillar MTJs. Challenges: metal redeposition after etch shorting the MgO sidewall, and the ~400 °C back-end limit affecting magnetic properties and TMR.
  • ReRAM: a metal oxide (HfOx, TaOx) between electrodes — simple, but needs a one-time forming step to create the initial filament.
  • PCM: chalcogenide deposition and etch are prone to damage and composition drift; the heater must shrink to lower RESET current.

Yield and process control

The simplest yield model assumes randomly, uniformly distributed defects (Poisson):

Y = e−A·D₀　　(A: critical die area, D₀: defect density)

Real defects cluster, so the negative binomial model Y = (1 + A·D₀/α)−α is common. Memory’s advantage is redundancy repair: random single defects in the array are fixed with spare rows / columns, so what really kills a die is periphery defects and clusters beyond the repair budget. Pre-repair yield can be low and post-repair yield much higher.

Example: A = 0.7 cm² and D₀ = 0.2 /cm² give an unrepaired Poisson yield of e−0.14 ≈ 87%; if 75% of the die is repairable array, the fatal area is only 0.175 cm² and yield is about 97% (ignoring cases where repair resources run out).

  • Inline monitoring: defect inspection (bright / dark field optical, e-beam), CD-SEM / OCD for line width and hole profile, overlay, film thickness.
  • WAT / PCM: test structures in the scribe lines (transistors, contact chains, capacitors, resistors) measured after wafer completion and watched with SPC for drift.
  • Feedback from test: CP fail bit maps can be traced to defect signatures of specific layers — whole-row failures to word-line shorts, regular checkerboard patterns to lithography problems. See Test & packaging.

Glossary

STI
Shallow trench isolation.
SADP / SAQP
Self-aligned double / quadruple patterning, halving / quartering pitch with sidewall spacers.
EUV
Extreme-ultraviolet lithography (13.5 nm wavelength).
ALD
Atomic layer deposition: layer-by-layer growth that coats deep holes uniformly.
Aspect ratio
Height-to-width ratio of a structure.
ZAZ
ZrO2/Al2O3/ZrO2 capacitor dielectric stack.
EOT
Equivalent oxide thickness in SiO2 terms.
Landing pad
A conductive pad above the storage-node contact that eases capacitor alignment.
VCT
Vertical channel transistor for 4F² DRAM.
ON stack
The alternating oxide / nitride stack of 3D NAND.
Replacement gate
Using SiN as a placeholder, then hollowing it out and filling metal word lines.
Staircase
The stepped structure used to contact 3D NAND word lines.
CuA / CBA
CMOS under Array / CMOS bonded Array.
Hybrid bonding
Direct bonding of Cu–Cu and dielectric at the same time, bumpless and at very fine pitch.
IBE
Ion beam etching, mainly physical sputtering, used for MTJs.
D₀
Killer defect density per unit area.
WAT / PCM
Wafer acceptance test / process control monitor structures.
SPC
Statistical process control.

Process details vary by maker; these are generic descriptions and orders of magnitude from public literature.
