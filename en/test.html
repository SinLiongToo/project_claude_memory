Chapter 5 · Test & packaging

Test & packaging: find bad bits, fix them, stack the dies

Memory is the most test-intensive semiconductor product: every bit of every chip must be written, read and verified at hot and cold temperatures; bad bits must be found and repaired, and unrepairable dies scrapped. Packaging has moved from single dies to TSV stacks a dozen layers high, raising the bar for known-good die (KGD).

The test flow

  • WAT (wafer acceptance test): measures scribe-line test structures to confirm process parameters are in spec — a process monitor.
  • CP (chip probe / wafer sort): a probe card contacts each die’s pads for DC tests (open/short, leakage, IDD) and functional tests, outputting each die’s failing-bit data.
  • Repair: assign spare rows / columns from the failing addresses, program them with laser or electrical fuses, then retest.
  • FT (final test): full-speed test on ATE after packaging, binning parts by the speed and timing they pass (speed binning).

Memory fault models

Testing is not “write something and read it back”. Possible physical defects are abstracted into fault models, and test sequences are designed to cover them.

March algorithms

A March test is a sequence of March elements. Each element walks every cell in a given address order (⇑ ascending, ⇓ descending, ⇕ either) and performs a list of operations on each (w0 write 0, r1 read expecting 1, …). Complexity is linear in the cell count N, which suits large memories.

March C−: { ⇕(w0); ⇑(r0,w1); ⇑(r1,w0); ⇓(r0,w1); ⇓(r1,w0); ⇕(r0) }　→ 10N

Why it is designed this way

  • Every cell is read as both 0 and 1 → catches SAF.
  • Every cell goes through 0→1 and 1→0 and is read afterwards → catches TF.
  • Walking both ascending and descending: for any pair of cells, in some element the aggressor is operated first and the victim read later → catches couplings in every direction.
  • Every element reads before it writes, expecting what the previous element wrote; a wrong address decode reads the wrong value → catches AF.

Repair flow and KGD

  1. CP1 runs the full test and records the address of every failing bit.
  2. Redundancy analysis (RA): each mat has a fixed number of spare rows / columns. If a row has more failing bits than spare columns, it “must” use a spare row (must-repair); the rest are assigned by search. Dies with no solution are unrepairable.
  3. Fuse programming: laser fuses (a laser cuts a metal link; needs an opening and works only on wafer) or electrical fuses / anti-fuses (blown or broken down electrically; also work after packaging).
  4. CP2 verifies the repair and runs cold or retention tests.

KGD (known good die): dies that will be stacked or co-packaged must reach near-final quality at wafer level, because one bad die drags down an entire expensive stack. That pushes high-speed and temperature tests, once done only at FT, into wafer test.

ATE and test cost

  • Parallelism: memory ATE tests hundreds of dies (CP) or packages (FT) at once, with an algorithmic pattern generator (ALPG) producing address and data sequences.
  • Test time: grows with density; retention tests must wait, which is especially slow. A full test of a multi-Gb die can take tens of minutes to hours.
  • Test cost can be a significant share of DRAM cost, so makers use on-chip DFT test modes (writing several banks at once, compressing compare results internally), drop redundant test items and raise parallelism.
  • DC tests: open/short (contact), input leakage, the IDD family (IDD0 activate current, IDD2P standby, IDD6 self refresh).
  • Speed binning: dies from one lot are shipped in grades by the highest speed and tightest timing they run reliably.

The same item is often repeated at several temperatures: hot catches leakage and retention, cold catches speed (contact and poly resistance rise when cold) and SA offset. Testing at only one temperature is a common source of escapes.

Shmoo plots and test margin

A shmoo plot sweeps two conditions (most often voltage and speed / timing) and marks each grid point pass or fail. It is the most used tool for characterization and debug, because its shape reveals the failure mechanism.

  • The slope: higher voltage means faster transistors, so the pass region usually has its top-left corner missing; the slope is the speed path.
  • Vmin wall: below some voltage everything fails however slow — usually the functional limit of SAs, SRAM or keepers.
  • High-voltage fails: fails at the high end can mean leakage, hot carriers, or circuits that race at high voltage.
  • Holes: scattered fails inside the pass region often mean pattern dependence, noise or marginal timing; confirm with other data backgrounds and repeated runs.
  • Temperature inversion: in advanced nodes at low voltage, cold is slower than hot (Vt rising in the cold outweighs better mobility), so the worst temperature at low voltage may be cold, not hot.

Guard bands and test correlation

Testers have measurement error, and test conditions never exactly match a customer system, so the test limit must be tighter than the spec. The difference is the guard band:

Test limit = spec − (tester timing accuracy + temperature / voltage difference + aging allowance)

Example: spec tRCD = 13.75 ns, tester edge-placement accuracy ±0.15 ns, plus 0.15 ns for temperature and aging → test at 13.45 ns.

  • Overkill: parts that meet spec but fail the tighter test limit and are scrapped. A bigger guard band means more overkill, lower yield and revenue.
  • Underkill / escape: parts that fail spec but pass test and ship. Too small a guard band, poor coverage or the wrong test temperature all cause it.
  • Correlation: results must agree across testers, test steps (CP vs FT vs SLT) and probe cards. Golden units (samples of known behaviour) are compared regularly and yield differences between sites are tracked.
  • Retest policy: does a failed part that passes on retest count as good? Retest recovers overkill from bad contact but can also let marginal parts slip through, so the count is limited and retest pass rates analysed.
  • Statistics: a parameter’s Cpk (process capability) sets the cost of its guard band; high-Cpk parameters can use smaller guard bands without more escapes.

Voltage tests: Vmin, Vmax, VLV, VHV

Voltage is the cheapest and most effective test knob. The four common voltage tests have different goals: Vmax and Vmin are margin tests that confirm the chip works across the spec voltage range; VLV and VHV are screening tests that deliberately go outside spec to expose latent defects.

All four tests take time, but it means different things. For Vmin, Vmax and VLV it is test time, which drives cost and coverage; for VHV it is stress time, which sets how much acceleration you get and whether healthy dies are damaged. Some flows combine the two as dynamic voltage stress (DVS): running test patterns at VHV so circuit nodes actually toggle, in which case time is again a stress parameter. For DRAM retention (pause) tests run at low voltage, the wait time is a key condition too.

Why VLV catches defects nominal voltage misses

  • At nominal voltage transistors drive hard, so a resistive bridge or open of a few kΩ only adds a little delay and the function stays correct. At low voltage drive current drops sharply, the same resistance becomes a large share of the delay or pulls a node into the wrong logic level, and the defect shows up.
  • These are often reliability defects: they work today but worsen in the field (electromigration, corrosion, thermal stress), so VLV screening directly cuts early field failures.
  • The VLV voltage must sit where every healthy die passes and defective ones fail. Too low and slow-but-healthy dies are overkilled; it is usually set from the healthy population’s Vmin distribution (e.g. 4–6σ above the mean) with a slower clock.
  • Very-low-voltage testing was proposed in the 1990s by Hao and McCluskey and others to screen weak CMOS devices.

How VHV stress works

  • Apply 1.3–1.5 × nominal for a short time (possible at wafer test) so defective, weak oxides break down early; then retest at nominal, Vmin and VLV to catch dies “converted” into hard failures.
  • Voltage usually accelerates far more than temperature: time to dielectric breakdown depends exponentially on field, so a few seconds at high voltage can replace a long burn-in, and VHV is often used to shorten or partly replace burn-in.
  • The risk is overstress: too high a voltage or too long a time damages healthy dies and leaves a “reliability debt”. The voltage limit must come from TDDB data, and healthy-die parameters must be verified unchanged after stress.
  • DRAM can stress internal supplies separately through test modes: raised VPP stresses the access-transistor gate oxide, a WL stress turns on all word lines at once, and a raised plate voltage stresses the capacitor dielectric.

Statistical uses of Vmin

  • Vmin outliers: a die’s actual Vmin is a sensitive health indicator. Dies with clearly higher Vmin than their lot often carry latent defects even if they pass spec, and can be removed with PAT (e.g. above median + 4σ).
  • ΔVmin: measuring Vmin before and after burn-in or HTOL; dies with large shifts are aging or defective, useful for screening and reliability analysis.
  • Vmin binning and AVS: logic chips are often binned by Vmin or use adaptive voltage scaling (AVS) in the system; SRAM Vmin usually sets the lowest operating voltage of a whole SoC.
  • Temperature: Vmin is often worst cold (temperature inversion), so Vmin and VLV tests should consider cold temperature.

Common pitfalls

  • Supply accuracy and droop: at low voltage, tens of mV of error is a large fraction. Measure close to the die pins (remote sense) and watch IR drop at high current.
  • VLV timing: dies are slower at low voltage; keeping full-speed timing overkills healthy dies.
  • Testing only spec corners: a Vmin test at the spec minimum barely catches resistive defects, because healthy and defective dies both pass inside spec — exactly why VLV exists.
  • No retest after stress: always rerun functional tests after VHV, or the failures it converts will ship.
  • External VDD vs internal supplies: DRAM has internal regulators, so changing external VDD may not change the array voltage; use test modes to adjust or monitor internal voltages directly (see internal supplies).

MBIST for embedded memories

An SoC has hundreds of SRAMs that external pins cannot reach directly. MBIST (memory built-in self-test) adds a controller beside each group of memories that generates March sequences on chip, compares the results and reports only pass/fail and failing addresses. With BIRA / BISR (built-in repair analysis / self-repair) spares are allocated automatically and the repair information stored in e-fuses, loaded at boot. Automotive chips also run MBIST at power-up or periodically in the field (in-system test) for functional safety.

Assembly flow

  1. Back grinding: thin the wafer from about 775 µm to tens of µm (multi-die stacks need very thin dies).
  2. Dicing: blade dicing, laser grooving or stealth dicing (a laser forms a modified layer inside the wafer, which is then expanded apart — good for ultrathin wafers). DBG (dicing before grinding) reduces chipping and cracking of thin dies.
  3. Die attach: fix dies to the substrate or the die below with die-attach film (DAF).
  4. Wire bonding: gold or copper wires connect die pads to the substrate; in multi-layer stacks dies are staggered or use film-over-wire (FOW).
  5. Molding: epoxy mold compound protects the assembly.
  6. Ball attach, laser marking, singulation.

HBM stacking and TSVs

  • TSVs: thousands of copper-filled through-silicon vias a few µm wide are etched into each DRAM die (usually between front and back end, via-middle); the wafer back is ground to reveal them and back-side bumps are added.
  • Die thinning: 12 layers plus a base die must fit JEDEC’s package height (720 µm for HBM3E, relaxed to 775 µm for HBM4), so each core die is only about 30–50 µm thick.
  • Stacking and underfill:
    • TC-NCF: non-conductive film between layers, thermo-compression bonded one layer at a time.
    • MR-MUF: all layers joined in one reflow, then liquid mold underfill fills the gaps — better heat dissipation and throughput.
    • Hybrid bonding: no bumps; direct Cu–Cu bonding shrinks layer gaps and total height, seen as key for stacks of 16 and more.
  • Test challenges: core dies get KGD tests before stacking; after stacking, TSV connectivity is tested and repaired with spare TSVs; the base die provides an IEEE 1500 test interface and MBIST. The finished stack (KGSD, known good stacked die) then goes to 2.5D packaging.
  • Heat and warpage: many thin dies have high thermal resistance, with a hot GPU next door; different expansion coefficients warp the stack and stress the joints.

Burn-in and outgoing quality

The bathtub curve says early failures (infant mortality) come from latent manufacturing defects. Burn-in runs chips for a long time at high temperature (e.g. 125 °C) and high voltage, accelerating weak parts into early failure so they are screened before shipping. Memory is burned in at wafer level (WLBI) or in burn-in ovens after packaging, often while running test patterns (test during burn-in, TDBI).

Outgoing quality is measured in DPPM (defective parts per million); automotive targets are often in single-digit DPPM or lower. Statistical methods such as PAT (part average testing) reject outliers that are within spec but clearly off their lot’s distribution. Qualification items are in Reliability.
