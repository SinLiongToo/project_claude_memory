Current temperature45 °CSA offset limit

Reading one bit in five steps

  1. Precharge: equalize BL and BLB to VDD/2.
  2. Word line on: the capacitor shares charge with BL, ΔV = (Vcell − VDD/2)·Cs/(Cs+CBL).
  3. SA amplify: if |ΔV| exceeds the SA offset, it amplifies in the right direction; otherwise it may go the wrong way.
  4. Restore: with WL still on, the amplified value is written back. On a misread the wrong value is written back too, and the data is permanently corrupted.
  5. Precharge: WL off, bit lines back to VDD/2.

Try this

  • Raise CBL and shrink Cs: the signal shrinks — that is why bit lines are kept short and capacitors tall.
  • Pick the “weak tail cell” and raise the temperature from 85 °C to 95 °C: it misreads within 64 ms. That is why refresh doubles when hot.
  • Lower VDD: ΔV shrinks proportionally, so low-voltage DRAM depends even more on low-offset SAs.
  • Read more: charge sharing, sense amplifiers.

Reading the butterfly curve

  • The two curves are the voltage transfer curves of the two inverters, one with Q as input, one with QB as input (drawn mirrored).
  • The upper-left and lower-right crossings are the two stable states (holding 0 / 1); the middle crossing is the unstable metastable point.
  • The side of the largest square in each “eye” is that state’s noise margin; SNM is the smaller of the two.
  • During a read the PG turns on and pulls the “0” node up, lifting the low end of the curve and shrinking the eye.

Try this

  • In Read mode lower β from 1.6 to 0.8: the eye almost closes and a read may flip the data.
  • Set ΔVt to ±80 mV: one eye grows and one shrinks, and SNM follows the smaller — that is how mismatch limits Vmin.
  • Look at SNM vs VDD: where read SNM falls to zero is roughly this cell’s read Vmin.
  • Read more: SNM, read/write assist.

Parameters charge-trap cell

Retention is converted to 25 °C equivalent time with Ea = 1.1 eV; high-Vt states lose more charge, and cycled cells lose it faster.

Vt distributions

Read levels in useFactory read levels

What the model does

  • Each state is a Gaussian. Its width comes from the ISPP step (quantization error ≈ step/√12), cell noise and traps from P/E cycling.
  • Retention pulls states toward a neutral voltage, and higher Vt drifts more, so upper states squeeze together and shift left.
  • RBER is the probability of landing in the wrong region; with Gray coding, crossing one read level costs one bit.
  • Hard-decision LDPC handles RBER around 10−3; beyond that it needs soft decoding or rereads.

Try this

  • TLC → QLC: twice the states, half the gaps, and much higher RBER under the same conditions.
  • Set P/E to 3000 and retention to 1 year: with fixed levels errors explode; switch to Read retry and most of it is recovered. That is why controllers calibrate read voltages.
  • Raise the ISPP step: faster programming (lower estimated tPROG) but wider distributions and more errors.
  • Read more: multi-level cells, flash retention.

Parameters DRAM die

Retention follows a mix of two log-normals: the main population (median ≈ 6 s at 85 °C) and a weak tail (median ≈ 0.5 s). Retention halves every 10 °C.

The trade-off

  • A shorter refresh window leaves fewer cells to leak out before being refreshed, but refresh takes more time and power.
  • Every 10 °C rise shifts the curve left by a factor of two in time; to keep the same failure fraction the window must halve.
  • Higher density means a longer tRFC and more overhead at the same window — the reason for DDR5 same-bank refresh.
  • Weak cells found in test are repaired with spare rows / columns, so the real goal is that no failing cell remains after repair at the worst-case temperature.

Try this

  • Go from 85 to 95 °C with the window unchanged: failing bits rise about tenfold. Press “Apply JEDEC rule” and see halving the window restore it.
  • Raise the weak-cell tail fraction: a worse process needs more repair resources or a shorter window.
  • Read more: refresh, DRAM retention.

Parameters one bank

In one refresh window a bank can be activated at most about tREFW ÷ tRC times (tRC ≈ 48 ns). Cell flip thresholds follow a log-normal; HCfirst is the weakest cell in the row.

Row state after one refresh window

Expected victim flips vs HCfirst

No mitigationTRRRFMPRAC

What the model does

  • The attacker activates n aggressor rows in turn within one refresh window; each gets 1/n of the total ACTs.
  • A victim accumulates the activations of its neighbouring aggressors (doubled when sandwiched between two) and flips when it exceeds a cell’s threshold.
  • TRR tracks only a few rows; with more aggressors than table entries, tracking thrashes and protection fails.
  • RFM: every RAAIMT activations the controller gives the DRAM time to refresh the tracked rows’ neighbours.
  • PRAC: every row has a counter and mitigation triggers at the threshold — the most complete protection, at a timing cost.

Try this

  • With TRR and a table of 4, change aggressors from 2 to 8 or 20: many-sided attacks bypass TRR — the idea behind TRRespass.
  • Lower HCfirst from 50K to 2K: scaling makes cells more fragile, and even RFM needs a lower threshold.
  • Compare the overhead of each mechanism: stronger protection costs more bandwidth.
  • Read more: Row hammer.

Notation

Try this

Parameters DRAM speed path

Speed follows an alpha-power model: delay ∝ μ(T)·V / (V − Vt(T))1.3. Vt rises when cold and mobility falls when hot, which produces temperature inversion. Spec: VDD 1.1 V ±5%, customer temperature 0–95 °C.

How to read it

  • The spec window (black box) is what customers will use; the test window (blue box) adds guard band and is what is actually tested — tighter than spec.
  • A part ships only if the whole test window passes. Passing the spec window but failing the test window = overkill.
  • The “all-temperature check” asks whether this die also meets spec at 0 °C and 95 °C; passing only at the test temperature is the escape risk of single-temperature testing.
  • The lower curves show temperature inversion: below about 0.94 V cold is slower, above it hot is slower.

Try this

  • Set the guard band to 0, then to 12%: see which dies move from “ship” to “overkill”.
  • TT corner, offset +2σ, test at 25 °C, timing guard band 0: the test passes but the all-temperature check fails at 95 °C — an escape. Set the guard band back to 4% and it is correctly rejected.
  • Test at −40 °C and watch the low-voltage region get slower (temperature inversion).
  • Read more: shmoo plots, guard bands, common test problems.

What the model does

  • Resistive defects raise Vmin, but most stay below the spec minimum, so the Vmin spec test barely catches them; VLV or Vmin PAT pull the limit down to the tail of the healthy distribution.
  • Latent oxide defects work at any voltage; only VHV stress converts them into detectable failures, and the conversion rises exponentially with voltage.
  • Too high or too long a VHV damages healthy dies — an invisible reliability debt counted in field failures.
  • Assumed field-failure probability: resistive escapes 50%, oxide escapes 60%, damaged healthy dies 30%, high-voltage-sensitive with a failure point inside spec 100%.

Try this

  • Only Vmin and Vmax: high yield but about 4,600 DPPM shipped defective. Turn on VLV (0.88 V): escapes drop sharply with almost no extra overkill.
  • Lower VLV to 0.84 V: more defects caught, but healthy dies start to be overkilled (watch overkill ppm).
  • Turn on VHV at 1.4×, 1 s: most latent oxide defects convert. Push to 1.6×: field failures soar — overstress.
  • Widen the healthy σ: the same VLV limit overkills more, so the limit must track the population.

Parameters 300 mm wafer

Defects per die are sampled from a gamma–Poisson (negative binomial). Defects in the array can be repaired with spare rows / columns; defects in the periphery are fatal.

Key points

  • At the same average defect density, more clustering (smaller α) gives higher yield: defects pile up on a few dies and leave the others clean.
  • Yield falls faster for larger dies — one reason large dies (HBM core dies, high-density DRAM) are expensive.
  • Memory redundancy “rescues” most array defects, so what really kills dies is periphery defects and clusters beyond the repair budget.

Try this

  • Set repairable defects to 0 and then back to 6: see how much repair recovers.
  • Switch α to ∞ (Poisson): yield is lower than with clustering at the same D₀ — why Poisson is usually pessimistic.

Parameters HBM stack

Escape rate = share of bad dies that pass the KGD test. Costs are relative: core die = 1, base die 1.5, stack assembly 2.

Stack sketch

Stack yield vs height

Why KGD matters so much

  • Stack yield is roughly the per-layer yield to the n-th power: 0.9512 ≈ 54%, 0.9912 ≈ 89%.
  • When a stack fails, every good die in it is scrapped too, so each extra layer tightens the quality required of every die.
  • Pre-test yield affects how many dies you consume; stack yield depends on whether passed dies are truly good, which test coverage decides.
  • TSV redundancy repairs a few bond failures and raises effective per-layer bond yield markedly.

Try this

  • Raise the escape rate from 3% to 15%, then go from 12 to 16 high: watch yield collapse.
  • Turn TSV repair off: at 99.5% bond yield, how much does it cost a 12-high stack?
  • Read more: HBM stacking and TSVs, repair and KGD.

Formulas

  • Dies per wafer DPW ≈ π r² / A − π d / √(2A).
  • Cost per good die = wafer cost ÷ (DPW × yield) + back-end cost.
  • Cost per Gb = cost per good die ÷ die density.
  • The core contest in memory is lowering cost per bit every generation (bit cost reduction): scaling adds bits per wafer while yield must ramp as fast as possible.

  • Switch to the HBM core die: a large die (TSVs take area) with similar density costs clearly more per Gb — and stack yield (HBM tab) adds even more.
