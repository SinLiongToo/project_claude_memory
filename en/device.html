Chapter 1 · Devices

Devices: how a bit gets stored

Every memory boils down to “use some physical state to mean 0 or 1”. SRAM uses a bistable circuit, DRAM uses charge on a capacitor, flash uses electrons trapped in a gate stack, MRAM uses a magnetization direction, and PCM uses the resistance of a crystalline or amorphous material. That choice decides speed, density, cost and whether the data survives power-off.

The memory hierarchy

No memory is fast, large and cheap at the same time, so computers stack them in a hierarchy: the closer to the processor, the faster, smaller and more expensive per bit; further down, larger and cheaper, but with latency orders of magnitude higher.

The hierarchy works because programs have locality: data used recently is likely to be used again soon (temporal locality), and nearby data is likely to be used next (spatial locality). If the cache captures that small hot set, average access time approaches SRAM while average cost approaches DRAM.

The 6T SRAM cell

SRAM (static RAM) uses two cross-coupled inverters as a bistable latch: when Q is 1, QB must be 0 and vice versa. As long as power is applied the state holds without refresh — that is what “static” means. Two access transistors (pass gates, PG) controlled by the word line WL connect the internal nodes to the bit lines BL / BLB.

The read / write tug-of-war

  • Read: BL and BLB are precharged to VDD. With WL on, the side storing 0 slowly discharges its bit line through PG→PD. The sense amp needs only tens of mV of difference. The danger is that during a read the PG lifts the “0” node; if it rises too far the latch can flip (read disturb). So the PD must be stronger than the PG — the cell ratio (β = PD/PG, commonly 1.5–2.5 in planar processes).
  • Write: the write driver pulls one bit line hard to 0 and, through the PG, drags the node holding 1 down, fighting the PU. So the PG must be stronger than the PU (the pull-up ratio).
  • Reads want a strong PD, writes want a strong PG — that conflict is the core of SRAM sizing. In FinFET/GAA processes fin counts are integers (e.g. 1:1:1 for high-density cells), so read/write assist circuits make up the difference; see Design.

Static noise margin (SNM)

Plot the transfer curves of both inverters, one mirrored, on the same axes and you get the “butterfly curve”. The side of the largest square that fits in its eyes is the SNM — how much noise voltage the cell tolerates without flipping. Read SNM is much smaller than hold SNM, and it shrinks as voltage drops and transistor Vt mismatch grows. That sets the minimum operating voltage Vmin.

Area and scaling

A 6T cell takes about 120–150 F² in a logic process — over twenty times DRAM’s 6F² — so SRAM is used only where speed matters most. High-density bitcells at 5 nm / 3 nm are around 0.02 µm². SRAM scaling has slowed sharply: when logic shrinks 1.6×, SRAM often shrinks only 1.0–1.2×, which is one reason 3D-stacked cache (SRAM dies on top of compute dies) has taken off.

Variants: 8T adds a separate read port (reads do not disturb the storage nodes, good at low voltage); dual-port / multi-port cells for register files; TCAM for network routing lookups.

The 1T1C DRAM cell

DRAM (dynamic RAM) uses one transistor and one capacitor per bit: a charged capacitor is 1, an empty one is 0. The structure is minimal, so density is high and cost low. The price is that capacitors leak and must be refreshed periodically — that is what “dynamic” means.

Charge sharing: why the signal is so small

The cell capacitance Cs is tiny (a few fF to around 10 fF), while hundreds of cells hang on the bit line, so its parasitic CBL is several times larger. When WL turns on, charge redistributes:

ΔV = (Vcell − VDD/2) · Cs / (Cs + CBL)

For VDD = 1.1 V, Cs = 10 fF and CBL = 50 fF, a stored 1 gives ΔV ≈ 0.55 × 10/60 ≈ 92 mV. That is why DRAM design obsesses over a few things: enough capacitance (hence extremely tall cylinder capacitors), short bit lines (hence many small mats) and low sense-amp offset.

Destructive read and restore

After charge sharing the capacitor sits near VDD/2 — the original data is destroyed. So while the sense amplifier drives the bit line to full swing, WL stays on and the correct value is written back into the capacitor (restore). That is why DRAM timing has tRAS, the minimum time from activating a row until it may be precharged.

Leakage and refresh

Stored charge leaks through several paths: junction leakage, sub-threshold leakage of the access transistor, gate-induced drain leakage (GIDL) and dielectric leakage. Most cells hold data for seconds, but a few “tail” cells last only tens to hundreds of milliseconds, so JEDEC requires every cell to be refreshed within 64 ms (32 ms for DDR5; halved again at high temperature). Retention roughly halves for every 10 °C rise.

6F² and the access transistor

Modern DRAM cells are 6F² (F is the feature size, e.g. 3F × 2F) with diagonally laid-out active areas. To keep leakage low in such a tiny footprint, the access transistor is a buried word line (buried channel array transistor, BCAT): the gate is buried in a silicon trench, the effective channel gets longer and leakage drops sharply. The next steps are 4F² vertical-channel transistors (VCT) and 3D DRAM; see Process.

Flash: locking electrons in the gate

A flash cell is a MOSFET with an extra charge-storage layer between the control gate and the channel. Injecting electrons raises the transistor’s threshold voltage Vt; reading applies a reference voltage to the control gate and checks whether the transistor conducts. The electrons are wrapped in insulators, so data lasts about ten years without power.

Program and erase

  • Fowler–Nordheim (FN) tunnelling: about 15–20 V on the control gate creates a field strong enough for electrons to tunnel through the oxide into the storage layer. The current is tiny and efficient; NAND uses it for program and erase.
  • Channel hot-electron injection (CHE): a high drain voltage creates energetic electrons that jump into the floating gate. Fast but power-hungry; NOR uses it to program.
  • Erase: a high voltage on the substrate (p-well) pulls electrons out. NAND erases a whole block at once, so data cannot be overwritten in place — which is exactly why SSD controllers need an FTL (see Applications).

3D NAND: building upward

Planar NAND hit its limit around 15 nm: cell-to-cell interference became too strong and only a few dozen electrons remained in a floating gate. The industry turned to stacking cells vertically. First deposit a hundred-plus alternating layers of insulator and word-line material, then etch channel holes through all of them at once and line each hole with blocking oxide, a SiN trap layer, tunnel oxide and a poly-silicon channel. Each crossing of a word-line layer and a channel hole is a gate-all-around charge-trap cell.

3D NAND density no longer comes from shrinking line width but from (1) more layers, (2) more bits per cell (TLC/QLC), and (3) moving the peripheral CMOS under the array (CMOS under Array) or onto a separately built wafer that is bonded on (CMOS bonded Array) to save die area. Process details are in Process.

Micron note　Micron and Intel’s early 3D NAND (the 32-, 64- and 96-layer generations) used a floating gate, and put the CMOS periphery under the array (CuA) from the very first generation. Micron later moved to replacement gate + charge trap (from roughly the 128 / 176-layer generations) and kept it for 232 layers and beyond. So “3D NAND is all charge trap” is not quite right — floating-gate 3D NAND shipped in volume.(Compiled from public sources, not official; see Micron’s own announcements for details.)

Emerging non-volatile memories

These technologies aim to fill the gap between DRAM and NAND (storage-class memory, SCM) or to replace embedded flash, which is increasingly hard to integrate in advanced nodes. Most are built between back-end metal layers (BEOL) and take no front-end transistor area.

MRAM (magnetoresistive)

The core is a magnetic tunnel junction (MTJ): two ferromagnetic layers (CoFeB) around a very thin MgO barrier. One layer’s magnetization is fixed (reference layer), the other can switch (free layer). Parallel means low resistance, antiparallel high; the difference (TMR) can exceed 150%. STT-MRAM switches the free layer with spin-polarized current through the MTJ; SOT-MRAM uses current in an adjacent heavy-metal layer, separating read and write paths for more speed and endurance. STT-MRAM is in production at 28/22 nm and below as an eFlash replacement and is common in automotive MCUs.

ReRAM / RRAM (resistive)

A metal–insulator–metal stack (e.g. TiN/HfOx/Ti). A voltage forms a conductive filament of oxygen vacancies (SET, low resistance); a reverse voltage breaks it (RESET, high resistance). Simple and CMOS-compatible, it is used as embedded NVM, and because resistance can take multiple levels it is studied for analog compute-in-memory. The challenge is resistance variation from random filament formation.

PCM (phase change)

A chalcogenide such as Ge2Sb2Te5 switches between amorphous (high resistance) and crystalline (low resistance): a short, strong pulse melts and quenches it amorphous (RESET); a longer, weaker pulse anneals it crystalline (SET). Intel/Micron’s 3D XPoint (Optane) was a cross-point PCM array with ovonic threshold switch (OTS) selectors. It was discontinued in 2022, demonstrating both the value and the commercial difficulty of SCM.

FeRAM / FeFET (ferroelectric)

Stores data as the polarization of a ferroelectric. Classic FeRAM uses PZT and scales poorly. In 2011 doped HfO2 (e.g. HfZrO2) was found to be ferroelectric at a few nanometres and CMOS-compatible, reviving 1T1C FeRAM and FeFETs (a ferroelectric layer inside the gate).

Glossary

F (feature size)
The process’s minimum half-pitch, used to normalize cell area as nF² across generations.
Bit line / Word line
The bit line (BL) carries data along a column; the word line (WL) selects the row being accessed.
Cell ratio β
Strength ratio of PD to PG in SRAM, which sets read stability.
SNM
Static noise margin: the side of the largest square that fits in the butterfly curve.
Vmin
The lowest supply at which SRAM reads and writes correctly, usually set by the worst few cells.
Cs / CBL
DRAM cell capacitance and bit-line parasitic capacitance; their ratio sets the signal after charge sharing.
Restore
Writing data back into a DRAM cell after a read, because the read is destructive.
Retention time
How long a cell keeps correct data without refresh.
GIDL
Gate-induced drain leakage: band-to-band tunnelling from the strong field where gate and drain overlap.
BCAT
Buried channel array transistor, the buried-word-line DRAM access device.
FN tunnelling
Quantum tunnelling of electrons through a triangular barrier under high field; the NAND program/erase mechanism.
CHE
Channel hot-electron injection, the NOR program mechanism.
ISPP
Incremental step pulse programming: raise the program voltage step by step with verifies to tighten Vt distributions.
VPASS / VREAD
Voltages on unselected / selected word lines during a NAND read.
Charge trap (CTF)
A flash cell that traps electrons in an insulating SiN layer; the 3D NAND mainstream.
MTJ / TMR
Magnetic tunnel junction and its tunnelling magnetoresistance; the core of MRAM and its read signal.
SCM
Storage-class memory: close to DRAM speed, potentially non-volatile, sitting between DRAM and storage.
